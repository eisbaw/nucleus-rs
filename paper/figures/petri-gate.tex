% fig:petri-gate --- the three unsound cases the soundness gate is built to
% reject, contrasted with an accepted net. Faithful to sec:arch-gate of
% chapters/06-architecture.tex and the negative falsifiers of sec:val-negative:
%   (a) sound: replay completes, no place exceeds capacity;
%   (b) boundedness violation: a producer overfills a finite channel;
%   (c) deadlock: a cyclic wait leaves every required transition un-fireable;
%   (d) free-choice conflict: one token, two competing consumers --- ruled out
%       BY CONSTRUCTION in emitted nets (the in-gate check is a tripwire).

  \caption[The sound case and the three the gate rejects]{One sound net and the
    three unsound shapes the soundness gate is built to reject. Circles are
    places, bars are transitions, filled dots are tokens. \textbf{(a)} A sound
    net: the channel's marking never exceeds its declared capacity, every
    required transition eventually fires, and the single replayed firing order
    runs to completion. \textbf{(b)} A \emph{boundedness} violation: the producer
    fires twice into a capacity-one channel before the consumer drains it, so the
    place would hold two tokens --- caught by the replay as a capacity overflow.
    \textbf{(c)} A \emph{deadlock}: two workers each wait on a channel the other
    must fill first; with both channels empty no transition is enabled and the
    replay stalls. \textbf{(d)} A \emph{free-choice conflict}: one place offers its
    single token to two enabled consumers, so which one consumes it is a
    nondeterministic choice. In the nets this compiler emits, case~(d) cannot
    arise --- the per-worker control places serialise each worker's transitions by
    construction (\cref{sec:arch-gate}) --- so the in-gate conflict check is a
    regression tripwire rather than the source of the guarantee; cases~(b)
    and~(c) are what the boundedness and deadlock replays catch on a genuinely
    unsound schedule.}
